Twenty years, one repeating question.

What actually drives someone to engage, stay, go deeper, or walk away? The room keeps changing — a mobile game, a fintech app, an NFL simulation played by millions. The question doesn't.

My first job out of college was dipping sample containers in a river — water chemistry for a water management district. I ended up building a Microsoft Access database for the data, and got so into it that I spent evenings at home in MS Paint making a pixel-by-pixel map of the St. Johns River for the loading screen. The computer work was clearly the part I actually wanted to be doing. I went back to school for a Master's in Mathematics, then spent fourteen years in software development, always finding my way to the data end of whatever the project was supposedly about.

Games are where it got interesting. At Zynga I built the company's first play-style clusters, and learned an early lesson I still carry: the segments I actually found weren't the clean archetypes anyone expected going in — people were arriving at the same behaviors through very different paths. I was the first data hire at Big Huge Games, helped build DomiNations into a $175M mobile strategy title, and did that "first person in, build the whole thing from nothing" move two more times — at the fintech startup Facet, then at EA Sports.

At EA Sports' Tiburon studio, a three-person team on one title grew into sixteen people supporting Madden and College Football. Along the way, at Facet, a COO once told me the data was "wrong" because she didn't like what it said. It wasn't wrong. That taught me trust in a data pipeline has to be airtight, not just correct — people will find any crack in the numbers to avoid an answer they don't want.

I left Tiburon this year. Same skill, next chapter: I want to point it somewhere the answer actually changes what happens next, not just how a store shelf gets arranged. That's what the tools I'm building now are about — see Projects.
