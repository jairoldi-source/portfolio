Right math + wrong question = wrong result.

Ask for average spend on the site, and you've already decided that's the right lens on the real question. We've moved from "I don't need data to decide" to "I must have data to decide" - but that's not the same as being data-informed. Data-informed means the analyst is in the room from the start, making sure the question is right, not just the math.

I've spent fifteen years or so trying to figure out what questions are worth asking of data. The easier part of an analytics job tends to be pulling the answers. The hard part is knowing which problem is actually worth solving, and then making sure the answer lands with the people who need to act on it.

My background is in games and fintech - industries I loved working in and learned an enormous amount from. Earlier this year I made a deliberate decision to step back and think carefully about what comes next, rather than just take whatever came along. I want my next chapter to be at an organization where the mission is genuinely about making things better for people who need it - not just making a product stickier or a funnel more efficient.

The analytical problems turn out to be surprisingly similar everywhere I've worked: what drives engagement, what causes people to stay or leave, what the aggregate number is hiding that only shows up when you look at the right population segment. At EA Sports, the aggregate said Madden and College Football had separate audiences. Split the data by segment and the real story was the opposite - the highest-value players were playing and spending in both, not choosing one over the other. That's the pattern that repeats: the monolithic number hides the group that actually matters.

I've built and rebuilt data functions from scratch a few times now - in a nine-person game studio, a hundred-person fintech startup, and a sports game franchise generating a billion dollars a year. What I've learned is that the infrastructure is never really the hard part. The hard part is changing what people believe data can do - moving an organization from counting things to deciding things, from describing what happened to actually shaping what happens next.

The things I've built on the side this year tend to start with a version of "this should exist and it doesn't." The Words That Work tool started because I read a research report about what messaging resonates with local news audiences and thought their PDF toolkit deserved to be a real, working tool. The Intake Tool came from wanting AI in the analytics process without losing what makes an analyst worth having. Unbounded AI reporting - point a model at the data, get conclusions back - is confidently incomplete: it looks exactly as polished whether the comparison group is valid or not. So instead of a tool that hallucinates a nice-looking SQL query, I built one that keeps a human in the loop - it helps analysts build out a knowledge base and teaches stakeholders how to frame requests that are actually answerable (see Projects).
