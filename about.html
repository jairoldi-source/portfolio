Fifteen years, one repeating question.

What actually drives someone to engage, stay, go deeper, or walk away? The room keeps changing — a mobile game, a fintech app, an NFL simulation played by millions. The question doesn't.

My first job out of college was dipping sample containers in a river - water chemistry for a water management district. I built a Microsoft Access database for the data because someone had to, and got so into the interface that I spent evenings at home in MS Paint making a pixel-by-pixel map of the St. Johns River for the loading screen, just because I wanted to. I went back to school for a Master's in Mathematics, then spent fourteen years in software development. No matter what a project was supposedly about, I always ended up on the data side of it.

Games are where it got interesting. At Zynga I built the company's first play-style clusters, and learned an early lesson I still carry: the segments I actually found weren't the clean archetypes anyone expected going in - people were arriving at the same behaviors through very different paths. I was the first data hire at Big Huge Games, helped build DomiNations into a $175M mobile strategy title, and then did that same move again, twice more - first person in, build the whole thing from nothing - at the fintech startup Facet, then at EA Sports.

At EA Sports' Tiburon studio, a three-person team on one title grew into sixteen people supporting Madden and College Football. Somewhere along the way, at Facet, a COO once told me the data was "wrong" - not because it was, but because she didn't like what it said. That's the lesson that stuck: trust in a data pipeline has to be airtight, not just correct. People will find any crack in the numbers to avoid an answer they don't want.

I left Tiburon this year. Same skill, next chapter - I want to point it somewhere the answer actually changes what happens next, not just how a store shelf gets arranged. That's what the tools I'm building now are about (see Projects).
