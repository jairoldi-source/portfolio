From "Give Me a Number" to "Help Me Decide"

An AI-assisted intake tool for analytics teams stuck fielding requests instead of shaping decisions.

The problem: stuck in reactive mode

Across several industries, I've seen analytics teams stuck in the same loop: reactive, pull-the-number mode, organized around however requests happen to arrive rather than what the business actually needs. When stakeholders go to whichever analyst they know best (or worse, send the same ask to two or three analysts at once hoping someone answers first) nobody is prioritizing with the full picture in view: every stakeholder group, the real business impact, the actual timelines. Analysts are on a work treadmill leading to no time to get to the deeper (more interesting and impactful) problems and eventually burnout.

I've built a few iterations of intake processes to move teams out of that loop and into a prioritized, sprint-based system instead. A stakeholder committee sets priorities, so analysts are working the highest-impact asks across the business rather than whoever asked loudest or most recently. Prioritization stays visible across groups as well so for example marketing can see why an analyst is heads-down on a product request this sprint, instead of wondering why their own ask seems to be sitting untouched. And a sprint gives analysts something reactive work rarely offers: a finite, achievable list for the period, not an infinite treadmill.

Getting analytics a seat at the table

Prioritization gets a team out of reactive mode. To get to a place where analytics is actually decision support not just metric supply the next step is changing the requests themselves to allow analysts to shape the analytics questions not just supply the answers.

In my experience stakeholders default to asking for a metric (e.g. "I need retention from the past two weeks") instead of naming the business decision they're actually trying to make. I coach analysts to push back on metric-first asks and ask the why behind the ask - sometimes referred to as climbing the "so what" ladder. From the retention question above we want to get the stakeholders to explain what they are looking to do with the information - e.g. "We're pitching an event targeted at competitive players starting in two weeks and we want to make sure there are still enough of them playing for it to be worth it." That's a far more layered question than "give me our retention metric" alone. It means knowing who typically participates in events like this, how they've historically heard about them, what current counts look like for that population - and yes, retention for that specific segment. Answer only the literal metric and skip the ladder, and I've seen it point stakeholders toward the exact opposite of what the real analysis would have shown.

Having those conversations with the analysts is also what teaches stakeholders that analytics is more than charts on a slide. It's how the team earns a seat in the room before the decision gets made, not a request for a chart after.

Where AI fits — and where it deliberately doesn't

There are a lot of thoughts on where AI belongs in the analytics process. From my perspective, there are a couple of essential pieces - first, don't use a probabilistic model as your means for answering deterministic questions and second, don't lose the parts of the thinking process that are unique to humans. AI doesn't know what it doesn't know and it will answer just as confidently regardless. Analysts bring the things AI genuinely can't: context, creativity, and the judgment to fill in the gaps a purely probabilistic process will always leave. So this tool intentionally stays strongly human-in-the-loop. AI does what it's actually good at - holding onto everything the team has learned, surfacing it as guidance, and putting a friendlier front end on the whole process - but does not ever make the analytics call itself.

Most Gen AI reporting efforts run into the same wall: AI is probabilistic, and analytics is deterministic. A hallucinated number is bad in a way that's hard to walk back. Even when it doesn't lead to an outright bad decision, it damages trust in the data itself, and that trust doesn't come back easily. So instead of pointing AI at the numbers and asking it to generate SQL, this tool points the AI at something where a wrong guess is cheap to catch and correct: the intake conversation, and the knowledge base behind it.

Every analytics team builds up scar tissue over time - the one analyst who knows the app went down last year on that particular Tuesday, so this year's comps need an asterisk, the folks who remember to include caveats for those small but essential user segments who consistently behave in unusual ways, etc. That knowledge usually lives in people's heads and scattered Slack threads, and it surfaces right when it's about to save (or quietly ruin) an analysis. This tool is built to catch that tribal knowledge as analysts teach it to the AI once, and put it in front of humans and AI alike the next time it's relevant.

How it works

For stakeholders

The tool gives you a plain-language place to actually talk through your analytics ask. If it's something the team has already answered, it can point you straight to the existing dashboard or past analysis, link included, with a quick how-to. For anything more involved, it walks you through a short, adaptive conversation about the business question behind your ask - not a static form, but real questions shaped by what you've asked and what's already in the knowledge base. From there, your request goes to an analyst. Once they've replied, you can keep the conversation going, mark it resolved, or - for anything that needs real analytics work - ask to have it prioritized into the sprint queue. You can check your own requests any time, or see what the whole team's queue looks like.

For analysts

The tool does two things: it helps turn a stakeholder's raw ask into something actionable through conversation, and it helps you build a knowledge base that both you and the AI can draw on later.

On the conversation side, the AI is prompted to walk stakeholders up the "so what" ladder itself by asking the probing questions, informed by what's already in the KB and you'll see the results waiting in your queue, ready for you to adjust and tighten before anything goes back to the stakeholder. Take as much or as little of the AI's draft as you want. It's built to point stakeholders at an existing dashboard, a past analysis, or a near-identical past request before proposing new work from scratch.

On the knowledge base side, the information behind all of this covers your segments, definitions, known events, past analyses, dashboards, and levers, plus the heuristics that shape how the AI reasons about a request. It's yours to edit any time from the left nav, and the tool actively nudges you toward it: mention a sale or a campaign in a request, and it'll suggest adding it as a known event; describe a new way of segmenting players, and it'll suggest a new segment. The more you build it out, the better the tool gets at framing requests, routing stakeholders to existing work, and suggesting your own next step - including a first-draft scoping document, pulled straight from the KB you've built.

None of this is meant to run without you. The analyst is the decision-maker. The AI is a structured interviewer and pattern-matcher against the knowledge base you build. The tool is meant to take the repetitive, rote parts of intake off your plate, so you can spend your time on the part AI can't do.
