Career journey.

It wasn't a straight line. Seven stops, a winding path, and the lesson each one left behind.

Water Management District → M.S. Math
The Beginning: Following the Data

I didn't plan to be a data person — I planned to work in environmental science, and my first job was scooping water samples out of a river in Florida. The part I loved was building the Microsoft Access database that stored the results; I was so proud of it I went home and built the loading screen in MS Paint, pixel by pixel. Other teams wanted their own version. I went back for a master's in math after that.

Lesson carried forward
Build for replication. The best systems outlast the person who built them.
IT Consulting · 1997–2011
The Long Runway: IT Consulting

About fourteen years building business applications. Data was always the back-end piece — the unglamorous part nobody else wanted. I did it anyway, and got good at it. The joint application design sessions I ran taught me to drive toward what people actually needed to decide, not just what they thought they wanted to build.

Lesson carried forward
Understanding someone's job is the prerequisite to making data useful for them.
Zynga · 2011–2013
Finding My People: Games and Analytics

I met someone at a party who ran a game company and thought it sounded like the most fun group of nerds I could work with. I was right — Zynga was an analytics company with a games veneer, data embedded in everything. My first project was building the company's first player archetypes; we expected classic MMO types and got something messier and more honest instead — Viral Askers, Spenders, Grinders. Not what anyone had imagined. They made it onto posters anyway, and changed how the product team thought about design.

Lesson carried forward
The finding that disappoints people is often the most useful one — and delivering it well is a skill worth developing.
Big Huge Games · 2013–2019
Building From Scratch: Games Strategy

Nine people, one game — I was the first data person and the first product manager, producing the analysis and consuming it at the same time. We built DomiNations, a $175M mobile strategy game still running today. When stakeholders wanted popularity rankings to cut low-volume items, I asked a different question: popular for whom? Those items looked expendable in aggregate but were critical to our most engaged players — the ones who'd played long enough to reach them.

Lesson carried forward
Aggregate metrics are a majority vote. The most important signal is almost always in the subgroup that gets averaged away.
Facet Wealth · 2019–2022
The Mission Problem: Fintech

I joined Facet to work somewhere with a mission — democratizing financial planning for people who'd never had access to it. I was the first data hire, building a production-grade stack from Excel and chewing gum in about six months. The harder lesson came later: a new leader wanted data to confirm her direction, not evaluate it. When we found the lead-generation strategy wasn't working, it landed as a threat, not information.

Lesson carried forward
Building trust in data is as important as building the data itself — small cracks in confidence become craters.
Electronic Arts / Tiburon · 2022–2026
The Biggest Build: Transforming a Studio

I walked into a team that had been leaderless for six months, two of five analysts gone before my first major launch — and we delivered more with a skeleton crew than the full team had the year before. I replaced an ad-hoc request system with a structured intake built around one question: what decision are you trying to make? The analyst who resisted it most vocally told me two years later he'd taken the system to his next team.

Lesson carried forward
When you build something people want to replicate, you've actually done the job.
Sabbatical · 2026
Now: The Intentional Pause

I left EA earlier this year to think carefully about what comes next, rather than take whatever came along. The things I've been building tend to start with "this should exist and it doesn't" — a tool that tests whether advocacy messaging actually hits the right frame, an intake system that uses AI to help analysts ask better questions instead of hallucinating SQL.

Lesson carried forward
Building things on the side clarifies what you actually care about faster than thinking about it does.

Data is most powerful when it's asking the right question — not when it's confirming the answer someone already had.
